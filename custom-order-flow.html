01 — THE PROBLEM

Customers kept stalling.

Every order relied on two printed pieces: a paper form and a 14-page catalog. Both slowed the line down.

THE PAPER FORM

  • Showed every option at once, in text only.
  • Had no field for a font or icon.
  • Let customers pick options their piece could not take.

THE PRINTED CATALOG · 14 PAGES

Cover
  • Customers flipped back and forth to compare pieces.
  • Too many pages to remember what each piece offered.
  • One copy meant one reader. Everyone else waited.

02 — VERSION 1

Put the rules in the flow.

Version 1 moved the order to the customer's phone. It asks one question at a time and shows only the options each piece can take.

03 — VERSION 2

Put the answer in the option.

VERSION 1 · SHARED SAMPLE

  • The font names came first, with one sample image below them.
  • Customers scrolled down to the sample, then back up to choose.
  • On a phone screen, the sample was too small to read.

VERSION 2 · SAMPLE IN EACH OPTION

  • Every option shows its own full alphabet.
  • Customers choose by looking, not by remembering a name.
  • Older relatives used it without any explanation.

04 — THE PHYSICAL SIDE

Put the position in the jig.

The F1 cannot show exact placement on its platform, and its guide beam disappears in direct sun. I laser-cut a jig for each piece, so it drops into position without long calibration.

06 — WHAT I LEARNED

Good UI and a solid UX process pay off at the booth.

Watching real customers order at markets showed me how much a clear interface saves. Every fix saved time for the customers, for me, and for the business.

01 · RECOGNITION OVER RECALLShow it, don't explain it.

Short labels and pictures cut the cognitive load, so customers choose without stopping to think. Each option shows what the finished piece will look like.

02 · SERVICE BLUEPRINTDesign the back end too.

I designed the backstage too: every order lands in one sheet and matches a ready-made template. I spend my time engraving, not sorting through notes.

03 · USABILITY TESTINGTest with the least techy user.

I ran usability tests with older relatives, timing each task and logging every pause. Each pain point became a change in the next version.

In the time one paper order used to take, I can now help 3–5 customers fill in the form at once.
